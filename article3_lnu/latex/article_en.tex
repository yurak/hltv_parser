\begin{Title}
IDENTIFICATION OF CONTEXT-INVARIANT PLAYER SKILL METRICS
IN ESPORTS THROUGH EFFECT-SIZE ANALYSIS
\end{Title}

\begin{Authors}
Yu.\,I.\,Kuzhii$^{1}$, Yu.\,M.\,Furgala$^{1}$
\end{Authors}

\addcontentschapter{Kuzhii\,Yu., Furgala\,Yu.}
{Identification of context-invariant player skill metrics in esports}

\begin{Organization}

Universytetska Str., 1, Lviv, 79000,\\

\begin{Abstract}
Assessing individual player skill in team-based esports is complicated by the dependence of
observed performance metrics on match context --- the particular map, the game version, and the
side played. This study examines the invariance of 46 Counter-Strike player performance metrics
across three dimensions, using 3486 player\,$\times$\,map observations (1820 from
Counter-Strike~2, period 2023--2025; 1666 from Counter-Strike: Global Offensive, period
2012--2023) collected from the public resource HLTV.org across eight maps. Invariance is defined
by effect size rather than by the $p$-value, because for samples of several thousand observations
the latter indicates significance even for practically negligible differences. One-way ANOVA with
$\eta^2$ (maps), the Mann--Whitney U test with Cohen's~$d$ (versions), and a paired $t$-test with
Cohen's~$d$ (sides) are applied. We find that 76.1\,\% of metrics are invariant to the map,
93.5\,\% to the game version, and only 47.8\,\% to the side, so that the side is the strongest
source of contextual bias. Sixteen metrics are simultaneously invariant across all three
dimensions; this ``triple-invariant'' set (overall rating, firepower, opening-duel and clutch
metrics, sniper multi-kills) reflects intrinsic player ability and is suitable for building robust
skill-assessment and scouting models. A sensitivity analysis confirms the robustness of the
conclusions within each game version: all 33 metrics that are map-invariant in CS2 remain
invariant in CSGO as well.
\KeywordsEng
esports, pattern recognition, feature invariance, effect size, feature selection,
skill assessment, analysis of variance.
\end{Abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Esports analytics increasingly relies on machine learning and pattern recognition methods to
assess individual player skill and to select the features that characterise
performance~\cite{Bishop}. Counter-Strike is one of the most popular team disciplines, and
detailed per-map statistical profiles of its players are publicly available. Observed performance
metrics, however, are shaped not only by a player's individual ability but also by match context:
the particular map, the game version (the transition from Counter-Strike: Global Offensive to
Counter-Strike~2), and the side played (attack --- terrorists, defence --- counter-terrorists).

Building robust skill-assessment models requires separating \textbf{context-invariant} metrics,
which reflect stable player ability, from \textbf{context-dependent} ones, which describe the
situation rather than the player. This formulation belongs to the classical feature-selection
problem~\cite{Guyon} and is consistent with the dataset-shift problem, in which the properties of
the feature space change between observation conditions~\cite{Quinonero}.

The aim of this work is to identify Counter-Strike player performance metrics that are invariant
with respect to the map, the game version, and the side, and to isolate the subset that is
invariant across all three dimensions simultaneously. Unlike the common approach based on
statistical significance, we define invariance through \textbf{effect size}, because at a sample
size of several thousand observations even practically negligible differences become statistically
significant~\cite{Cohen,Sullivan}.

\section{Materials and methods}

\subsection{Dataset}

The data were collected from the public resource HLTV.org --- per-map statistical profiles of
players. Each observation corresponds to a player\,$\times$\,map pair and contains an aggregated
profile of metrics overall and separately for the CT and T sides. After merging the two game
versions, filtering valid maps (names with the \texttt{de\us{}} prefix) and discarding records
without a version label, \textbf{3486 observations} were obtained (CS2 --- 1820, period
2023-09-27\,\ldots\,2025-12-31; CSGO --- 1666, period 2012-08-21\,\ldots\,2023-09-26) for
\textbf{8 maps}: \texttt{de\us{}ancient}, \texttt{de\us{}anubis}, \texttt{de\us{}dust2},
\texttt{de\us{}inferno}, \texttt{de\us{}mirage}, \texttt{de\us{}nuke}, \texttt{de\us{}train},
\texttt{de\us{}vertigo}.

\textbf{Forty-six baseline (overall) performance metrics} were analysed, corresponding to the
seven play-style attributes of the HLTV taxonomy~\cite{HLTV}: firepower, entrying, trading,
opening duels, clutching, sniping, and utility usage. All numeric columns were cast to a numeric
type with correct handling of missing-value markers.

\subsection{Definition of invariance}

A metric is considered \textbf{invariant} with respect to a dimension if the effect size of the
between-group differences does not exceed the moderate threshold of Cohen's
classification~\cite{Cohen}. $p$-values are reported for reference but are not used as a
criterion, because at $n \approx 3486$ they almost always indicate significance regardless of the
practical magnitude of the differences~\cite{Cohen,Sullivan}.

\subsection{Statistical methods}

\textit{Maps (8 groups, independent).} One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with effect size
$\eta^2 = SS_{between} / SS_{total}$. Invariance threshold: $\eta^2 < 0.06$ (below a medium
effect).

\textit{Game version (2 groups, independent).} The non-parametric Mann--Whitney U test (robust to
the departures from normality and to the outliers typical of game statistics) with effect size
Cohen's $d = |\mu_1 - \mu_2| / \sigma_{pooled}$, where
$\sigma_{pooled} = \sqrt{(\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2)/2}$. Threshold: $d < 0.5$. Note that the test
is rank-based whereas Cohen's~$d$ is a parametric measure; we combine them deliberately in order
to keep a single effect-size scale across all three dimensions, while acknowledging that a
rank-based measure (Cliff's $\delta$, for instance) would be strictly more consistent.

\textit{Side (2 groups, paired).} Every player has paired CT and T observations within the same
profile, so a paired $t$-test was applied with the paired
Cohen's $d = |\mu_{diff}| / \sigma_{diff}$ (the within-subject form). The threshold values are
borrowed from Cohen's conventions for independent samples; for the paired $d$ this makes the
effect-size estimates somewhat more conservative, which should be taken into account when
interpreting the side dimension specifically.

\textit{Handling of missing values.} Each test uses the observations available for the
corresponding metric (pairwise deletion), so the sample size depends on the metric: for most
metrics the share of missing values did not exceed 1\,\%, reaching 9--21\,\% only for derived
sniper percentage metrics (for example, \texttt{1on1\us{}win\us{}percentage} has
$n_{CS2} = 1754$; for the paired side analysis \texttt{1on1\us{}win\us{}percentage} yields 3026
pairs against 3486 for complete metrics). All $n$ values are recorded in the output tables.

\textit{Dimensionality reduction.} Principal component analysis (PCA) on the standardised overall
metrics, used to visualise the separability of groups by map and by version~\cite{Jolliffe}.

\textit{Intersections.} The ``universally invariant'' set is the intersection of the map- and
version-invariant metrics; the ``triple-invariant'' set adds the intersection with the
side-invariant ones.

\textit{Sensitivity analysis.} Because the game version is a potential confounder in the map
analysis (the data are pooled), map invariance was additionally computed separately within each
version.

All computations were carried out by reproducible scripts (Python: pandas, numpy, scipy,
scikit-learn, matplotlib); the output tables and figures are generated automatically.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Invariance to the map}

Of the 46 metrics, \textbf{35 (76.1\,\%)} are invariant to the map ($\eta^2 < 0.06$). Invariance
is highest for the opening-duel metrics (\texttt{opening\us{}deaths\us{}per\us{}round},
\texttt{opening\us{}attempts}) and for 1-on-1 duels
(\texttt{1on1\us{}win\us{}percentage}), all with $\eta^2 < 0.001$. The strongest map dependence is
found in utility-related metrics: \texttt{utility} ($\eta^2 = 0.247$),
\texttt{utility\us{}damage\us{}per\us{}round} ($\eta^2 = 0.243$),
\texttt{flashes\us{}thrown\us{}per\us{}round} ($\eta^2 = 0.209$). The distribution of effect sizes
across all three dimensions is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:inv}.

\subsection{Invariance to the game version (CS2 vs CSGO)}

\textbf{Forty-three of the 46 metrics (93.5\,\%)} are invariant to the version
(Cohen's $d < 0.5$). The most stable are \texttt{opening\us{}deaths\us{}per\us{}round}
($d = 0.003$), \texttt{utility} ($d = 0.004$), \texttt{last\us{}alive\us{}percentage}
($d = 0.015$). The largest differences appear in team-interaction metrics:
\texttt{assisted\us{}kills\us{}percentage} ($d = 2.51$; 27.1\,\% in CS2 against 17.8\,\% in CSGO),
\texttt{assists\us{}per\us{}round} ($d = 1.80$), \texttt{support\us{}rounds} ($d = 0.96$). The
distributions of key features for the two versions are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:dist}.

\subsection{Invariance to the side (T vs CT)}

The side has the strongest influence: only \textbf{22 of the 46 metrics (47.8\,\%)} are invariant.
The most stable are \texttt{opening\us{}attempts} ($d = 0.001$), \texttt{entrying}
($d = 0.005$), \texttt{pistol\us{}round\us{}rating} ($d = 0.009$). The most side-dependent are
\texttt{saves\us{}per\us{}round\us{}loss} ($d = 1.96$),
\texttt{trade\us{}kills\us{}percentage} ($d = 1.00$),
\texttt{saved\us{}by\us{}teammate\us{}per\us{}round} ($d = 0.98$),
\texttt{flashes\us{}thrown\us{}per\us{}round} ($d = 0.97$). A comparison of the mean values by
side is given in Fig.~\ref{fig:side}.

The intersection of the three dimensions yields \textbf{33 universally invariant} metrics
(map + version) and \textbf{16 triple-invariant} ones (map + version + side), listed in

\subsection{PCA and sensitivity analysis}

The first two principal components explain 23.1\,\% and 18.2\,\% of the variance (41.3\,\%
together). In the PCA projection the groups by map and by version overlap substantially
(Fig.~\ref{fig:pca}), which visually confirms the predominant invariance of the feature space. The
sensitivity analysis produced similar shares of map-invariant metrics within each version ---
71.7\,\% (CS2, 33/46) and 76.1\,\% (CSGO, 35/46) against 76.1\,\% on the pooled data.
Importantly, these shares are a quantity distinct from the share of the universally invariant set
(also 33/46), with which they coincide numerically. The sets are stable in composition: all 33
metrics that are map-invariant in CS2 are also invariant in CSGO (the difference amounts to two
additional metrics in CSGO: \texttt{assisted\us{}kills\us{}percentage},
\texttt{attacks\us{}per\us{}round}), so pooling the versions does not distort the conclusions
about maps.

\section{Discussion}

The results outline a clear hierarchy of contextual influence: the weakest is the game version
(7\,\% of variant metrics), moderate is the map (24\,\%), and the strongest is the side
(52\,\%). This means that for cross-context skill assessment the side is the decisive source of
bias, and metrics should either be normalised by side or restricted to the side-invariant set.

The pattern of results is consistent with game logic. The strong map dependence of utility usage
is explained by the different grenade strategies that different maps require, whereas the
stability of the opening-duel metrics reflects individual skill in the first engagements. The
predominant invariance to the version indicates that basic mechanical skills transfer between
CSGO and CS2, while the largest differences are concentrated in assist and support metrics, which
may be related to the mechanical changes introduced in CS2; this statement is correlational and
requires separate verification. The dependence of save and utility metrics on the side matches
the different economic and positional tasks of CT and T.

The triple-invariant metrics mostly concern individual aiming and decision-making skills in key
situations (first engagements, clutches, pistol rounds) rather than team coordination (assists,
support, saves), which is expected to depend on role and side. This makes them natural candidates
for features in skill-assessment and scouting models.

Compared with the common approach based on statistical significance, defining invariance by
effect size yields substantively different results: at $n \approx 3486$ the overwhelming majority
of metrics differ ``significantly'' ($p < 0.05$) even for negligible differences, so the
significance criterion is not applicable here.

\textit{Limitations.} (1)~The data are aggregated at the map level, without distinguishing
opponents or a player's period of form. (2)~The CS2 period is shorter than the CSGO one, which may
induce a shift in the player pool. (3)~Some sniper percentage metrics have up to 21\,\% missing
values. (4)~The analysis is correlational; causal statements about game mechanics require
additional verification. (5)~Confidence intervals for $\eta^2$ and Cohen's~$d$ were not computed;
adding them (by bootstrap, for instance) would strengthen the central argument about effect sizes.

\section{Conclusions}

1.~Context-invariant performance metrics of Counter-Strike players were identified on a set of
3486 observations across three dimensions on the basis of effect sizes.

2.~Invariance holds for 76.1\,\% of the metrics with respect to the map, 93.5\,\% with respect to
the game version, and 47.8\,\% with respect to the side; the side is the strongest source of
contextual bias.

3.~Sixteen triple-invariant metrics were isolated; they reflect intrinsic player ability and are
suitable for robust feature selection.

4.~The advantage of the effect-size criterion over statistical significance was demonstrated for
large samples; the sensitivity analysis confirmed the robustness of the conclusions within each
game version.

5.~A prospective direction is the application of the triple-invariant set in skill-assessment
models and in the detection of anomalous player behaviour patterns, as the next stage of the
publication series.
